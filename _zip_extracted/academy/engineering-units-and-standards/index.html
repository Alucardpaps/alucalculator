Engineering Units and Standards (SI vs Imperial)

Engineering is a quantitative discipline. Without standardized units, global collaboration and manufacturing would be impossible. In this module, we explore the International System of Units (SI) and the Imperial system, focusing on how to convert between them accurately. Modern supply chains span continents — a drawing in millimeters must mate with tooling calibrated in inches. The SI system (meter, kilogram, second, ampere, kelvin, mole, candela) is the international reference, while Imperial units persist in US aerospace and legacy machinery. This lesson establishes the conversion discipline used throughout AluCalc: exact constants, consistent significant figures, and explicit unit labels on every intermediate result. Every AluCalc kernel assumes SI internally. This lesson trains you to spot mixed-unit traps before they reach a drawing or BOM — the same discipline NASA mandates after the Mars Orbiter loss.

Learning objectives

  1. Convert between SI and Imperial length, mass, and force units without ambiguity
  2. Identify when mass (kg) must not be substituted for force (N) in equilibrium equations
  3. Apply ISO/DIN rounding rules for manufacturing tolerances expressed in microns
  4. State assumptions and validity limits
  5. Apply the governing equation with SI units
  6. Connect the result to a design decision and inspection plan
Engineering tipAlways use SI units for internal calculations and only convert to Imperial for final presentation if required.

Why This Matters

  • Incorrect unit conversion led to the loss of the Mars Climate Orbiter.
  • Manufacturing tolerances are often specified in microns (μm), requiring high precision.
  • Deep understanding here prevents expensive late-stage redesign and field failures.

Common Mistakes

  • Using decimal points incorrectly in different locales (comma vs point).
  • Mixing mass (kg) and force (N) units in calculations.
  • Neglecting temperature unit shifts (Celsius to Kelvin).
  • Copying results without restating assumptions and units.
  • Optimizing for one limit state while ignoring another (e.g. strength vs stiffness vs fatigue).

Technical Q&A

What is the base unit of length in SI?

The meter (m).

Why is 25.4 mm/in exact?

The inch was redefined in 1959 so that 1 in = 25.4 mm exactly — use this value, not 25.0, for precision fits.

Can I use kg and N interchangeably?

No. kg is mass; N is force (kg·m/s²). Weight in newtons equals mass times local gravity.

When should I convert to Imperial?

Convert only at presentation boundaries — carry SI internally through all calculation steps.

How do I know my assumptions are still valid?

Check regime limits (elastic vs plastic, laminar vs turbulent, thin-wall, small deflection). If any assumption is violated, switch models or escalate to FEA/test.

What belongs in the calculation package?

Inputs with units, sketches/FBDs, equations, numeric steps, allowables, safety policy, revision/effectivity, and linked standards.

How should I use the AluCalc calculator with this lesson?

Reproduce the worked example first, then change one variable at a time to build intuition for sensitivity and design trade-offs.

Key takeaways

  • Always carry SI internally; convert only at presentation boundaries
  • 1 in = 25.4 mm exactly — never approximate for precision fits
  • Unit errors propagate silently until they cause field failures
  • Units and regimes are part of the answer
  • Standards close the loop between theory and shop floor
  • Document for the next engineer — including future you
