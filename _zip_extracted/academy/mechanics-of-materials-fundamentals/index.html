Mechanics of Materials: Stress, Strain, and Deformation

While Statics deals with external forces, Mechanics of Materials looks inside. How do materials respond to those forces? Do they stretch, bend, or break? This course defines the internal resistance of solid bodies to external loads. Once reactions are known from statics, you must ask whether the material survives. Normal stress σ = F/A and Hooke's law ε = σ/E link force to deformation. Stress concentrations at holes and fillets can locally exceed nominal values. Every strength calculator in AluCalc — from fasteners to beams — builds on this σ–ε foundation. This deepened edition adds explicit objectives, review documentation expectations, and failure-mode thinking so the lesson behaves like a full design module—not a quick formula card. Statics gives external reactions; strength of materials tells you whether the material survives inside. Every stress calculator in AluCalc builds on σ = F/A and ε = σ/E.

Learning objectives

  1. Compute normal stress σ = F/A and interpret tension vs compression
  2. Apply Hooke's law to relate stress, strain, and elastic modulus
  3. Identify stress concentrations at holes, fillets, and sudden section changes
  4. State assumptions and validity limits
  5. Apply the governing equation with SI units
  6. Connect the result to a design decision and inspection plan
Engineering tipIn engineering design, we usually target a Factor of Safety (FoS) between 1.5 and 3.0.

Step-by-Step Calculation

  1. 1Calculate the internal force at the point of interest.
  2. 2Determine the cross-sectional area of the element.
  3. 3Apply the stress formula: σ = F / A.
  4. 4Calculate strain using Hooke's Law: ε = σ / E.
  5. 5Verify that stress is below the material's yield strength.
  6. 6Apply stress concentration factor Kt when geometry has discontinuities.
  7. 7Compare computed stress to yield with an appropriate factor of safety.
  8. 8List governing standards and company design rules that apply to this problem.
  9. 9Perform a unit and order-of-magnitude sanity check before finalizing.
  10. 10Document assumptions, inputs, outputs, and residual risks for design review.
  11. 11Identify the manufacturing/inspection implication of the calculated result.

Why This Matters

  • Prevents structural failure by ensuring material limits are never exceeded.
  • Allows for the selection of appropriate materials based on loading conditions.
  • Deep understanding here prevents expensive late-stage redesign and field failures.

Common Mistakes

  • Neglecting the sign of the stress (Tension vs Compression).
  • Using the wrong area for shear stress (it's the area parallel to the force).
  • Failing to account for stress concentrations at holes or fillets.
  • Copying results without restating assumptions and units.
  • Optimizing for one limit state while ignoring another (e.g. strength vs stiffness vs fatigue).

Technical Q&A

What is the difference between stress and pressure?

Pressure is an external force distributed over an external surface, while stress is an internal resistance distributed over an internal cross-section.

What is factor of safety?

Ratio of allowable to computed stress — typically 1.5–3.0 depending on consequence of failure and load uncertainty.

How is shear stress different?

Shear acts parallel to the area; area is the section in the plane of shear, not perpendicular to force.

When does Hooke's law fail?

Beyond proportional limit / yield — plastic deformation requires different constitutive models.

How do I know my assumptions are still valid?

Check regime limits (elastic vs plastic, laminar vs turbulent, thin-wall, small deflection). If any assumption is violated, switch models or escalate to FEA/test.

What belongs in the calculation package?

Inputs with units, sketches/FBDs, equations, numeric steps, allowables, safety policy, revision/effectivity, and linked standards.

Key takeaways

  • Stress is internal resistance; pressure acts on external surfaces
  • Stay below yield with an appropriate factor of safety (1.5–3.0)
  • Shear area lies in the plane of the shear force — not the normal area
  • Units and regimes are part of the answer
  • Standards close the loop between theory and shop floor
  • Document for the next engineer — including future you
